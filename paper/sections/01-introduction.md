# 1. Introduction

That animals vary in their personalities has long been accepted [@Slater1981], and this concept has gained traction in farmed animals [@finkemeier2018] alongside a renewed emphasis on consistent individual differences in animal welfare research [@Richter2019]. This shift in focus from the group to the individual recognizes that identical environments can affect animals within a group differently [pigs: @lawrence1991individual; beef cattle: @creamer2024consistent; dairy cattle: @Brasier2023].

In most dairy production systems in the Northern hemisphere, lactating cows are housed intensively, managed in groups, and fed a total mixed ration (TMR) distributed once or twice daily [@DeVries2003]. Although farms aim to maximize milk production through precision feeding and improved dry matter intake (DMI), the DMI of group-housed cows is shaped by the feeding behavior of individuals within the group, which is in turn modulated by environment, management, health, and social interactions [@grant1995feeding]. Cattle exhibit personality-related variability in feeding behavior [reviewed in @neave2018personality] and develop different strategies to cope with social and environmental challenges [@Foris2021].

Traditional personality tests, which often involve isolating animals for choice and motivation tasks [@dawkins2021], are increasingly criticized because they may not reflect natural behavior in group settings [@biro2012], and existing measures are fraught with inconsistencies [@finkemeier2018]. The same limitations apply to studies of social competition at the feed bunk, which have historically relied on live observation or video coding [e.g., @phillips2001effects; @faerevik2010effect; @Huzzey2012]. Both approaches are labor-intensive and require extensive training to achieve acceptable intra- and inter-observer reliability [@huzzey2014automatic].

Precision Livestock Farming (PLF) technologies offer the opportunity to quickly and continuously record measures of feeding and social behavior, which enables analyses that are simply not feasible with manual observation. Electronic feeders such as the Insentec Roughage Intake Control (RIC) system (Hokofarm Group, Marknesse, The Netherlands) have been validated [@chapinal2007validation] and are now used at research facilities worldwide [New Zealand: @biswas2022methane; United States: @allwardt2017technical; Canada: @huzzey2014automatic; Germany: @Foris2021]. While the primary purpose of these systems is to monitor feed intake, each visit is also automatically logged with its location, start time, and duration [@chapinal2007validation]. From these basic data streams, researchers have derived additional behavioral measures, including displacements at the feed bunk [@huzzey2014automatic], dominance relationships [@sheng2024redefining], and synchronous feeding [@Foris2021]. ecent work in cattle has begun to apply multi-level modeling to these data streams to quantify behavioral type (an individual's average expression of a behavior), repeatability (consistent among-individual differences), and predictability (individual differences in within-individual consistency) [dairy cows: @thomas2024; dairy calves: @carslake2022b; @occhiuto2022].

Despite the growing adoption of electronic feeding systems and the analytical frameworks built around them, two practical gaps limit how researchers can use these data. First, there is no standardized, open-source tool for converting raw electronic feeder and drinker output into analysis-ready behavioral variables. Each research group implements its own cleaning rules and thresholds, which limits reproducibility and makes cross-study comparisons difficult. Second, most published analyses rely on a small set of basic metrics: typically daily intake and feeding duration, even though the raw data contain far richer information. Higher-resolution variables that connect detections across time and location can capture, for example, how feed availability along the bunk evolves through the day, when and where a cow chooses to eat relative to that availability, whether she visits the feeding area without consuming feed (non-nutritive visits), and how often she switches bins within a meal. These variables are computable from data that many facilities already collect. What is missing is the tooling to extract them consistently.

In this paper, we address both gaps. Specifically, we:

1. **Introduce `moo4feed`, an open-source R package for processing electronic feeder and drinker data.** The package applies distribution-based thresholds tuned to the user's own dataset to clean raw records and derive both standard measures (intake, visits, meals) and novel behavioral variables (non-nutritive visits, bin switches per meal, displacements inferred from short inter-visit intervals).
2. **Quantify individual-level behavioral consistency and among-individual differences** across the variables produced by the package, using repeatability and predictability to identify which behaviors are most informative for characterizing individual differences.
3. **Demonstrate the practical value of the pipeline** by using the most consistent among-individual differences to identify distinct behavioral profiles within the herd, and by presenting individual behavioral narratives for representative animals from each profile.

Together, these contributions provide both the software infrastructure and a worked example for extracting individual-level behavioral information from electronic feeding systems, with applications in health monitoring, welfare assessment, and personality research.
