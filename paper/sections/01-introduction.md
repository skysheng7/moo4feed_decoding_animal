# 1. Introduction

That animals vary in their personalities has long been accepted [@Slater1981], but this concept has recently gained traction in farmed animals [@finkemeier2018] given the renewed emphasis on understanding consistent individual differences when addressing animal welfare issues [@Richter2019]. This shift in focus from the group to the individual recognizes that identical environments can affect animals within the group differently [pigs: @lawrence1991individual; beef cattle: @creamer2024consistent; dairy cattle: @Brasier2023]. 

In most dairy cattle production systems in the Northern hemisphere, lactating cows are housed intensively and managed in groups and fed a total mixed ration (TMR) that is distributed once or twice daily [@DeVries2003].  Dairy farms aim to maximize milk production through precision feeding strategies and improved DMI. However, the DMI of group-housed lactating dairy cows is also affected by feeding behaviors expressed by the individuals within the group, which is modulated by the environment, management, health, and social interactions [@grant1995feeding].

Cattle exhibit personality-related variability in feeding behavior [reviewed in @neave2018personality] and develop different strategies to deal with social and environmental challenges [@Foris2021]. Traditional personality tests, which often involve isolating animals for choice and motivation tasks [@dawkins2021], are increasingly criticized as they may not reflect animals’ natural behavior in group settings [@biro2012] and current measures are fraught with inconsistencies [@finkemeier2018]. In contrast, data-driven approaches using longitudinal sensor derived measurements have recently been used to identify personality traits by observing animals in their home pen environments with herd mates [@carslake2022a; @setser2024b]. However, a comprehensive framework for automatically characterizing individual social and feeding strategies has yet to be elucidated. Development of such a framework would allow us to routinely characterize individuals in groups and identify those who might be more susceptible to distress due to maladaptive strategies. 

The influence of the social environment on individual personality traits in animals has received some attention [see review by @webster2011personality], but this work has relied on conventional techniques to record competition involving either live observation or video recordings [e.g., @phillips2001effects; @faerevik2010effect; @Huzzey2012]. In addition to being very labour intensive, both require considerable training as achieving high intra and inter observer reliabilities can be challenging [@huzzey2014automatic]. Identifying faster and more objective ways to measure longitudinal competitive behavior at the feed bunk that also allows for data capture would be useful. 

Recent advances in animal behavior modelling approaches [@hertel2020] provide a useful framework for studying cattle behavior based on continuous automated data streams. One example of an electronic data source that fits within this analytical framework in the cattle research context is the use of electronic feeding systems. The Insentec Roughage Intake Control (RIC) electronic feeder and drinker system (Hokofarm Group, Marknessee, The Netherlands) has been validated [@chapinal2007validation] and is now used by many research facilities worldwide [e.g., New Zealand: @biswas2022methane; United States: @allwardt2017technical; Canada: @huzzey2014automatic; Germany: @Foris2021]. While the primary use of this system is to monitor feed intake, the generated data also allows us to capture associated feeding behaviors such as the feeding location, frequency, and duration of visits [@neave2018personality] as well as social behaviors [i.e., displacements at the feed bunk: @huzzey2014automatic; dominance: @sheng2024redefining; synchronous feeding: @Foris2021]. 

Recent studies in cattle have used longitudinal data streams from various sensors and applied novel multi-level modelling approaches to quantify behavioral types (an individual’s average expression of certain behaviors) and measure repeatability (consistent among-individual differences), and predictability (individual differences in within-individual consistency) which help understand variation in individual behavioral baselines and normal deviations [dairy cows: @thomas2024; dairy calves: @carslake2022b; @occhiuto2022]. However, the majority of this research [i.e., @thomas2024] has prioritized simple metrics such as daily feeding behaviour captured from neck-mounted sensors to investigate repeatability and predictability of the measure. Expanding computational capacities beyond these simple measures will allow us to make use of existing data streams to derive higher-resolution and more complex metrics that connect the detections across locations and take the entirety of available information into account for each second of the day feed availability, such as how much food is available to eat and where along the feed bunk is it available; when and where does the cow choose to eat, and does her decision depend on how much food is available and where along the feedbunk is it available. Also, are there times that she accesses the feeding area but chooses not to consume feed (null visit).  These decisions made by the cow(s) are also affected by the social environment – how individual cows vary in their social and feeding behaviour as they access food along the feedbunk.

Personality has been defined in terms of consistent responses in behaviour of individual animals across contexts and over time [@reale2007]. Longitudinal data streams have yet to be incorporated into personality assessment; indeed it has been argued that inclusion of these novel behavioral measures could revolutionize personality assessments in farm animals [@setser2024b]. Thus, the primary aim was to quantify individual-level behavioral consistency and among-individual differences in dairy cattle feeding and social behaviours captured using an automatic feeding system. Specifically, we were interested in using repeatability and predictability measures to identify which behaviors are most informative for characterizing individual differences. A secondary aim was to identify distinct behavioral narratives using the most consistent among-individual differences which could be incorporated into personality assessments. 





Cave paintings from 45,000 years ago show early depictions of humans and pigs [@mcdermott2021], providing evidence that humans have shared a long history of caring for animals. For our ancient ancestors, understanding animal behavior was no doubt central to informing animal care [@breed2021]. This early interest in animal behaviors included observing animals' movements, social interactions, cognition, and learning [@breed2021]. There is also evidence that many ancient civilizations used changes in behavioral signs to identify ill animals under their care [@griffith1898]. By the 1800s, naturalists like Charles Darwin were using behavioral observations to infer animals' affective states, demonstrating that animals express emotions such as fear, pleasure, and anger through their behavior [@darwin1872; @fraser2025].

Fast forwarding to the 20th century, in the industrialized world, the production of food from animals had intensified, largely driven by mechanization that reducee number of people required to care for a growing number of livestock [@rollin2011animal]. Although automation was integrated into many aspects of livestock farming, the monitoring of animal health continued to rely on the accumulated experience of human care givers  through manual behavioral observations [@hornbuckle1992]. These observations focused on identifying sickness behaviors associated with general malaise, such as reduced food and water intake and decreased activity [@hart1988biological]. Veterinary medicine has also adopted the approach of providing treatment and manage health at the herd level rather than the individual level [@medeiros2022historical]. These traditional approaches to identifying sick animals shared a common limitation: they typically considered the behavior of animals as a species or population rather than recognizing the importance of individual differences.

More recently, there has been a paradigm shift toward understanding individual differences in behavior [see @neave2020long; @weary2009]. This shift acknowledges what individual animals housed in identical environments exhibit remarkably different behaviors and strategies, even when healthy, due to their distinct personalities [@carslake2022a; @neave2020long]. Previous research also emphasized the need to assess animal welfare at the individual rather than herd level, since it is individual animals, not herds, that could suffer [@fraser2025].

Recent advancement of Precision Livestock Farming (PLF) technologies such as electronic feeders and drinkers offer new opportunities to automatically and continuously monitor animals on individual level, particularly behavioral and physiological measures [@dittrich2019]. This continuous monitoring is especially valuable for prey species, which often hide signs of illness when humans are present [@weary2009]. PLF has been widely promoted as tools for early disease detection [@dittrich2019]. However, much of the PLF research to date has focused on between subject comparison at the group level, such as comparing a specific behavior between healthy and sick groups [@cramer2015; @goharshahi2021]. This population-level thinking fails to account for the substantial individual differences in behavioral expression that is increasingly being documented in the literature [@carslake2022a]. These consistent behavioral differences across time and context reflect distinct animal personalities [@neave2020long].

Also of interest is that as animals become sick, the magnitude and direction of their behavioral changes vary dramatically between individuals; a phenomenon that has been attributed to personality differences [@setser2024a]. For instance, when comparing lying behavior in 'fearful' versus 'active' dairy calves with diarrhea, fearful calves reduced their lying bouts while active calves increased them [@setser2024a]. This personality-based variability extends to other behaviors as well. Foe, a recent study found that calves differ greatly in the consistency of their space-use behavior: some calves use a consistent amount of space across days, while others show dramatic day-to-day variation [@occhiuto2022]. Interpreting such behavioral changes requires understanding each animal's baseline patterns. For highly variable individuals, substantial changes in space use may be normal, whereas for consistent individuals, the same degree of change could signal health problems.

Although PLF has the ability to monitor animals individually, their full potential cannot be realized if data analysis and decision-making are made based on group-level averages. The key to effective disease detection lies in first understanding and tracking each animal's healthy behavioral patterns, then identifying when they deviate from their personal baseline. Recent studies have used multi-level models to understand individual behavioral baseline, quantify behavioral type (an individual's average expression oain behaviors), repeatability (consistent among-individual differences), and predictability (individual differences in within-individual consistency) (elephants: [@hertel2020]; dairy cows: [@thomas2024]; dairy calves: [@carslake2022b; @occhiuto2022]). However, existing research has focused primarily on simple metrics such as feed or water intake and feeding or drinking duration.

In the current study, we demonstrate that existing PLF systems can extend beyond these basic metrics, as these technologies capture rich data that can be used to derive novel behavioral variables. For instance, electronic feed bins could provide data on feed bin switches per meal as a measure of exploratory behavior [@meagher2017]. Additionally, agonistic interactions at feed bins can be identified when the interval between one cow exiting and the next cow entering is exceptionally short, suggesting that the incoming cow physically displaced or socially threatened the departing cow [@huzzey2014automatic; @foris2019automatic]. The ability to include novel behavioral measures could also revolutionize personality assessment in farm animals [@setser2024b]. Traditional personality tests, which often involve isolating animals for choice and motivation tasks [@dawkins2021], are increasingly criticized as they may not reflect animals' natural behavior in group settings [@biro2012]. In contrast, data-driven approaches using longitudinal PLF measurements enable researchers to identify personality traits by observing animals in their home pen environments with herd mates [@carslake2022a; @setser2024b].

In this study, we aim to:

**1. Introduce an R package for extracting behavioral variables from electronic feeder and drinker data.** We developed the `moo4feed` R package to enable researchers to reliably clean and process raw electronic feeder and drinker data using distribution-based thresholds customized to their own dataset. The package extracts both standard feedingnd drinking measures (e.g., intake, visits, and meals) and novel behavioral variables (e.g., non-nutritive visits, feed bin switches per meal), revealing the rich behavioral information already embedded within existing PLF data streams.

**2. Quantify individual-level behavioral consistency and among-individual differences.** Using repeatability and predictability measures, we assess which behavioral variables exhibit stable among-individual differences and which are characterized by greater within-individual variability, thereby identifying behaviors most informative for characterizing individual behavioral profiles.

**3. Identify distinct behavioral profiles through cluster analysis.** Using behaviors that exhibit consistent among-individual differences, we group cows with similar behavioral types to reveal the diversity of behavioral profiles present within a herd. To illustrate the practical value of this approach, we present individual behavioral narratives for one representative animal from each cluster, demonstrating how data can be used to tell each animal's unique story.

This research lays the groundwork for individual-based behavioural monitoring systems, which might improve health prediction, affective state assessment, natural behavior monitoring, and personality assessment.
