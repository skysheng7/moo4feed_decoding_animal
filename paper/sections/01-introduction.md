# 1. Introduction

That animals vary in personalities has long been accepted [@Slater1981], and this concept has gained traction in farmed animals [@finkemeier2018; @schwanke2025graduate] and in animal welfare research [@Richter2019]. The shift in focus from group to individual recognizes that identical environments can affect animals differently [pigs: @lawrence1991individual; beef cattle: @creamer2024consistent; dairy cattle: @Brasier2023]. In most dairy production systems in Canada, lactating cows are housed intensively, managed in groups, and fed a total mixed ration (TMR) distributed once or twice daily [@DeVries2003]. Although farms aim to maximize milk production through precision feeding and optimal dry matter intake (DMI), the DMI of group-housed cows is shaped by the feeding behavior of individuals within the group, which is in turn modulated by the environment, management, health status of the individuals, and social interactions [@grant1995feeding]. Cattle exhibit personality-related variability in feeding behavior [reviewed in @neave2018personality] and develop different strategies to cope with social and environmental challenges [@Foris2021].

Personality has been defined as consistent responses in the behavior of individual animals across contexts and over time [@reale2007]. Most traditional personality tests work by isolating animals to assess their choices and motivations [@dawkins2021]. The problem is that how an animal expresses its personality can look quite different depending on whether it is alone or in a social group [see reviews by @webster2011personality]. This has led to growing criticism of traditional personality testing approaches: not only because they may fail to capture how animals actually behave in social settings [@biro2012], but also because the measures themselves tend to be inconsistent [@finkemeier2018]. Studies of social competition at the feed bunk have also faced methodological challenges. These studies have historically relied on live observation or video coding [@phillips2001effects; @faerevik2010effect; @Huzzey2012], which are labor-intensive and require training to achieve acceptable intra- and inter-observer reliability [@huzzey2014automatic]. Sensor-derived measurements, by contrast, enable continuous and automated behavioral recording, reducing both the labor demands and the subjectivity inherent in human observation [@carslake2022a; @setser2024b]. 

Data from electronic feeding systems may be well-suited to this approach. For example, the Insentec Roughage Intake Control (RIC) system (Hokofarm Group, Marknesse, The Netherlands) has been validated [@chapinal2007validation] and is used at research facilities worldwide [e.g., Canada: @huzzey2014automatic; United States: @allwardt2017technical; Germany: @Foris2021; New Zealand: @biswas2022methane]. The primary purpose of these systems is to monitor feed intake, but each visit is automatically logged with its location, start time, and duration [@neave2018personality]. From these data, researchers have derived additional behavioral measures, including replacements at the feed bunk [@huzzey2014automatic], dominance relationships [@sheng2024redefining], and synchronous feeding [@Foris2021].

Recent work has begun to apply multi-level modeling to longitudinal data streams from animal data loggers [@hertel2020] to quantify behavioral type (an individual's average expression of a behavior), repeatability (consistent among-individual differences), and predictability (individual differences in within-individual consistency) [dairy calves: @carslake2022b; @occhiuto2022; dairy cows: @thomas2024]. However, most of this research has prioritised simple metrics such as daily feeding duration captured from neck-mounted sensors [e.g., @thomas2024]. The raw data from electronic feeders contains more information, including how feed availability along the bunk changes over time, when and where a cow chooses to eat relative to feed availability, whether she visits the feeding area without consuming feed (non-nutritive visits), and how often she switches among feed bins within a meal. These measures may capture aspects of individual feeding and social strategies that daily behavioral summaries cannot.

To date, no comprehensive and accessible framework exists for extracting these higher-resolution behavioral variables from raw electronic feeder and drinker data. A framework that automates this extraction would allow individual animals to be characterized routinely within groups. Data cleaning approaches also differ among labs and are rarely shared in a reusable form, which limits reproducibility across studies and routine application of these measures in dairy research [@wilkinson2016fair].

Our primary aim was to quantify individual-level behavioral consistency and among-individual differences in dairy cattle feeding and social behaviors captured from an electronic feeding system; we used repeatability and predictability to identify which behaviors were most informative for characterizing individual differences. Our secondary aim was to identify distinct behavioral profiles based on the most consistent among-individual differences, and to demonstrate how these measures could characterize feeding and social strategies present within a herd. To facilitate broad and reproducible application of the approach for researchers with varying technical backgrounds, we developed moo4feed (<https://www.skysheng.io/moo4feed/>), an easy-to-use open-source R package that processes raw electronic feeder and drinker data, and extracts both standard and higher-resolution behavioral variables.
