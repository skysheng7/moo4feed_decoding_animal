# 1. Introduction

That animals vary in their personalities has long been accepted [@Slater1981], and this concept has gained traction in farmed animals [@finkemeier2018; @schwanke2025graduate] alongside a renewed emphasis on consistent individual differences in animal welfare research [@Richter2019]. This shift in focus from the group to the individual recognizes that identical environments can affect animals within a group differently [pigs: @lawrence1991individual; beef cattle: @creamer2024consistent; dairy cattle: @Brasier2023]. In most dairy production systems in the Northern hemisphere, lactating cows are housed intensively, managed in groups, and fed a total mixed ration (TMR) distributed once or twice daily [@DeVries2003]. Although farms aim to maximize milk production through precision feeding and improved dry matter intake (DMI), the DMI of group-housed cows is shaped by the feeding behaviour of individuals within the group, which is in turn modulated by environment, management, health, and social interactions [@grant1995feeding]. Cattle exhibit personality-related variability in feeding behaviour [reviewed in @neave2018personality] and develop different strategies to cope with social and environmental challenges [@Foris2021].

Personality has been defined as consistent responses in the behaviour of individual animals across contexts and over time [@reale2007]. Most traditional personality tests work by isolating animals to assess their choices and motivations [@dawkins2021]. The problem is that how an animal expresses its personality can look quite different depending on whether it's alone or in a social group [see review by @webster2011personality]. This has led to growing criticism of traditional personality testing approaches: not only because they may fail to capture how animals actually behave in social settings [@biro2012], but also because the measures themselves tend to be inconsistent [@finkemeier2018]. The same limitations apply to studies of social competition at the feed bunk, which have historically relied on live observation or video coding [e.g., @phillips2001effects; @faerevik2010effect; @Huzzey2012]. Both approaches are labor-intensive and require extensive training to achieve acceptable intra- and inter-observer reliability [@huzzey2014automatic]. In contrast, data-driven approaches using longitudinal sensor-derived measurements allow researchers to characterize behaviour by observing animals in their home pens with herd mates [@carslake2022a; @setser2024b]. It has been argued that incorporating these continuous data streams could automate and revolutionize personality assessment in farm animals [@setser2024b; @schwanke2025graduate].

Electronic feeding systems are well-suited to this approach. The Insentec Roughage Intake Control (RIC) system (Hokofarm Group, Marknesse, The Netherlands) has been validated [@chapinal2007validation] and is used at research facilities worldwide [e.g., New Zealand: @biswas2022methane; United States: @allwardt2017technical; Canada: @huzzey2014automatic; Germany: @Foris2021]. While the primary purpose of these systems is to monitor feed intake, each visit is also automatically logged with its location, start time, and duration [@neave2018personality]. From these basic data streams, researchers have derived additional behavioural measures, including replacements at the feed bunk [@huzzey2014automatic], dominance relationships [@sheng2024redefining], and synchronous feeding [@Foris2021].

Recent work has begun to apply multi-level modeling to longitudinal data streams from animal data loggers [@hertel2020] to quantify behavioural type (an individual's average expression of a behaviour), repeatability (consistent among-individual differences), and predictability (individual differences in within-individual consistency) [dairy cows: @thomas2024; dairy calves: @carslake2022b; @occhiuto2022]. However, most of this research has prioritized simple metrics such as daily feeding duration captured from neck-mounted sensors [e.g., @thomas2024]. The raw data from electronic feeders contain considerably more information: how feed availability along the bunk evolves through the day, when and where a cow chooses to eat relative to that availability, whether she visits the feeding area without consuming feed (non-nutritive visits), and how often she switches bins within a meal. These higher-resolution measures may capture aspects of individual feeding and social strategies that daily behavioural summaries cannot.

To date, however, no comprehensive and accessible framework exists for extracting these higher-resolution behavioural variables from raw electronic feeder data. Data cleaning rules also differ across labs and are rarely shared in a reusable form, which limits both reproducibility across studies and the routine application of these measures in dairy research. A framework that automates this extraction would allow individual animals to be characterized routinely within groups, including those who may be more susceptible to distress due to maladaptive feeding or social strategies.

The primary aim of this study was to quantify individual-level behavioural consistency and among-individual differences in dairy cattle feeding and social behaviours captured from an electronic feeding system; we used repeatability and predictability to identify which behaviours are most informative for characterizing individual differences. A secondary aim was to identify distinct behavioural profiles based on the most consistent among-individual differences, to demonstrate how these measures can be used to characterize the diversity of feeding and social strategies present within a herd. To make these analyses reproducible and to enable broader application of the approach across species and researchers with varying technical backgrounds, we developed moo4feed (https://www.skysheng.io/moo4feed/), an easy-to-use open-source R package that processes raw electronic feeder and drinker data, extracts both standard and higher-resolution behavioural variables.
